# Jazz piano comping in a medium jazz waltz: a study from published sources

Notation: a bar of 3/4 is six eighths, `1 & 2 & 3 &`.
`x` = a strike, short (staccato or a stab, about an eighth); `X` = a strike marked with an accent; `-` = the strike before it is still held; `.` = nothing.
"Two bars" are written `bar1 | bar2`.

How much is known: the published evidence for *piano* comping in 3/4 is thinner than for 4/4. Most teaching pages on the subject are paywalled (Piano With Jonny's two "Jazz Waltz Rhythm Essentials" courses, Steve Herberman's masterclass, the TrueFire waltz lessons) or name the figures without notating them. The strongest evidence is two published transcriptions that I read bar by bar: Wynton Kelly comping on Miles Davis's "Someday My Prince Will Come" (1961), and the Bill Evans Trio's "Alice in Wonderland" (1961). Most of the figures in the table rest on Kelly alone, so they describe what one great pianist did on one record. They are not a rule.

**Checked afterwards (the engineer, against S1's PDF):** the quoted passages are in the paper as given. Of the score, bars 61 to 68 and 69 to 76 were read again from the page: the grids below agree in 14 of 16 bars. The two that do not: bar 69 has a right-hand chord on beat 1 held the bar (the "structural downbeat") over the left hand's `. X - - - x`, so it is a bar struck on 1; bar 70's left hand is `. . . x - -` (the "and" of 2 held through 3, inside the bar), not a strike on the "and" of 3 tied over the barline, so F10 has no instance and 3.1's one tied push is unconfirmed. The grids read the left hand; the right hand also plays melodic answers (bars 70, 72). Take the figures and the rough proportions from this study, not rates to the bar.

## Findings by question

### Q1. Where the piano's strikes fall in a medium waltz

**1.1 (solid: transcription, with S4 agreeing on the placements).** Behind a soloist, Kelly strikes about **two times a bar** and **leaves the downbeat out more often than he plays it**. In Davis's first 16 solo bars (bars 69-84 of S1, ♩=168), I counted 31 strikes in 16 bars (1.9 a bar, range 1-4). Only 6 of the 16 bars have a strike on beat 1. No bar is empty, but bars 78-80 are marked "Space (listening) L.H. voicings only". The bars as read:

| 70 | `. . . . . X-` | "and" of 3 tied over the barline into bar 71 |
| 71 | `x . . . . X` | |
| 72 | `. x . x . x` | all three "ands", short |
| 73 | `. . . x . .` | |
| 74 | `x . . . x .` | |
| 75 | `. X x x . x` | "dense voicings to emphasize the end of the 8 bar phrase" (S1) |

| 78 | `. . x - - -` | half note on 2 |

Struck positions, counted over those 16 bars: beat 1 in 6 bars, the "and" of 1 in 6, beat 2 in 5, the "and" of 2 in 6, beat 3 in 5, the "and" of 3 in 7.
S4 (Walsh) teaches the same two off-beats as "the 2 upbeats that make jazz waltzes swing". In his words: "shifting the chord from beat two up to the and-of-one … preserves the 3-beat pulse while introducing that off-beat accent that makes it swing", then "The second essential rhythm is playing on the and-of-two", mixed "sometimes hitting the and-of-one, sometimes the and-of-two, with occasional stabs right on beat 2 or 3", with "the bass on beat one" so "we hear that cycle clearly".

**1.2 (solid: transcription).** The **dotted-quarter cell**: beat 1 held a dotted quarter, then the "and" of 2 (`x - - x . .`). This is the commonest single figure when Kelly builds density. In bars 93-99 (S1: "Kelly gradually increases the density in his voicings, building tension in order to emphasize the arrival to the next structural downbeat"), every bar is struck on 1, and 5 of the 7 are this cell or a variant:
- 93 `x - - x . .`
- 94 `x - - - - -`
- 95 `x - - x . .`
- 96 `x - - x . X`
- 97 `x X . . x .`
- 98 `x - - x - -` (the second strike held to the barline: the bar is split into two dotted quarters)
- 99 `x - - x - x` (reading less certain)

The same cell opens the head (bar 37, the bar before the melody's A: `x - - x . .`) and appears in bar 77.

**1.3 (solid: transcription).** **One chord laid in and held through the bar** is Kelly's texture in the bridge of the head and at the section that releases tension. In the head's B section (bars 45-51), most bars are a single chord held a dotted half (`x - - - - -`). In bars 61-63, three bars in a row, the chord is struck on the "and" of 1 and held to the end of the bar: `. x - - - -`. Bars 64-68 are whole-bar held chords, "higher and more dense voicings (upper structure triads) to emphasize the arrival to the next structural downbeat". In the intro (bars 5-12, over the bass's F pedal) the voicings are long, held over two bars, with small eighth-note movements on beat 3.

**1.4 (solid: transcription).** Bill Evans **lays out under LaFaro's bass solo** on "Alice in Wonderland": the piano staff is dropped from those pages of S2's transcription (thesis p. 135). Under his *own* solo, Evans's left hand plays two to four chords a bar: off-beats, quarter-note triplets across the bar, chords tied over beats (S2, pp. 126 ff.). That is self-accompaniment, not comping for another player, and it is not offered here as a figure for the book.

**1.5 (thin).** S5 on Evans's "Someday My Prince Will Come" (*Portrait in Jazz*): "During the head in, the bass is walking in quarter notes, the drums are accenting beat two, and Bill Evans plays staccato chords outlining the harmony." No figure is given.

### Q2. Two against three, and hemiola

**2.1 (solid: transcription with the transcriber's own analysis; S9 names the device).** **Three half notes over two bars**: a short chord every two beats. This is Kelly's figure *behind the melody* of the A sections, sustained for 6 to 8 bars at a time. S1, p. 29: "During the A sections (mm. 37-44, and 53-60), Kelly plays a punctuated short chord every two beats, implying a 4x4 meter on top of the original 3x4 meter that we hear in Cobb's brush pattern and Davis' interpretation of the melody … All this tension is resolved at the arrival to the B (m. 45) and C (m. 61) sections."
As read from the score:
- bars 38-43: `x . . . x . | . . x . . .` three times, every strike a staccato quarter (about an eighth long)
- bar 44 closes on `x . . X - -`: the "and" of 2 tied through beat 3, as the turn into the bridge
- bars 53-60 run the same device in the other phase, starting `. . x . . . | x . . . x .`

So in this performance the hemiola is a *section's* figure. It belongs to the A sections of the head, and it stops at the section boundary.
S9 (Herberman) lists "the use of half notes to create 'over the bar line' phrasing" on jazz waltzes. His course is paywalled, so no figures are available from it.

**2.2 (solid as a device; how often is thin).** **Dotted quarters across the bar (2 against 3).** Kelly splits a bar into two dotted quarters in bar 98 (1.2), and his dotted-quarter cell (1.2) is half of that figure.
On the bass side, S2 quotes Chuck Israels, who played bass with Evans in the 1960s: "Sometimes, if I were playing a piece in ¾, I would play two dotted quarter notes, or I would play four quarter notes in a ¾ measure. And if I laid them in there as an obvious polyrhythm, three against four, that was playing time to me. And that was the way that Bill Evans thought about it, too." S2 finds that LaFaro "only sparingly employs the dotted quarter polyrhythm" on "Alice in Wonderland", and that the examples are "brief".
S8 advises: "A bar or two in the middle of a walking chorus is enough to make the point." S7 adds that some players feel a waltz "in two-bar groups, feeling six beats as a larger shape", a grouping that "does not automatically make it 6/8".
No source gives a rate of 2:3 bars for the piano.

### Q3. Anticipation: the "and" of 3

**3.1 (thin: one transcription, counted by me).** Kelly strikes the "and" of 3 in 7 of the 16 solo bars (1.1). One of those strikes is notated **tied over the barline** into the next bar (bar 70 into 71), which is a true push. Others are short (69, 72, 75) or accented at a phrase's end (84). In the head, bar 44's "and" of 2 tied through beat 3 leads into the bridge.
No source states how often a pianist anticipates the next bar's chord on the "and" of 3 in a waltz, or compares that rate with 4/4's "and" of 4. S1 says of 4/4 only that Kelly "tends to anticipate the chord at the 'and' of 4 to set up the phrases".

### Q4. Under a soloist against the head; quiet against loud

**4.1 (solid: transcription with the transcriber's commentary).** Kelly's texture follows the **form**, not only the volume. In the head he uses the A-section hemiola of short chords (2.1), held whole-bar chords in the bridge (1.3), and a held "and" of 1 at the release (1.3). Under the solo he plays varied one-to-four-strike bars and conversation (1.1).

**4.2 (solid: transcription with commentary).** **At the end of an 8-bar phrase he plays denser, louder, and higher.** S1 annotates "Kelly plays at a louder dynamic to emphasize the end of the 8 bar phrase" (bar 52: `x . . . x .`), "Dense chord voicings to emphasize the end of the 8 bar phrase" (bar 75: four strikes), and the build in bars 93-100 (1.2). S1, p. 28: "approaching the end of an 8-bar phrase, he tends to use thicker voicings in order to emphasize the arrival to the next structural marker".

**4.3 (solid: transcription with commentary).** **He answers the soloist in the gaps and plays sparsely under the line.** S1, p. 27: "when he is listening and waiting for the soloist to leave space, he shows a preference for simple two to four left-hand voicings (Appendix 1, mm. 78-80), then responding during the space". He uses "longer rhythmic values when Miles plays diatonically and rhythmically sparce … and simpler voicings and in short punches when the soloist plays harmonically and rhythmically complex long phrases, such as John Coltrane on 'Someday My Prince Will Come'". He also puts "melodic statements between Davis' phrases" (bars 70, 72, 85-88). Kelly himself, quoted in S1: "In general, I like to stay out of a man's way."

**4.4 (solid).** **Leaving the downbeat to the bass.** Under the solo, 10 of 16 bars have no piano strike on beat 1 (1.1), while Chambers holds a root on every downbeat (7.1). S4 makes the same division: the bass on one, the chord on the "and" of 1 or the "and" of 2.

**4.5 (gap).** No source describes the piano's register moving between head and solo, beyond Kelly's "higher and more dense voicings" at section arrivals (1.3).

### Q5. Named players

- **Wynton Kelly**, "Someday My Prince Will Come" (Miles Davis, Columbia, 1961; recorded March 1961 with Chambers and Cobb, S14): see 1.1-1.3, 2.1, 3.1 and 4.1-4.4 (S1). This is the best-documented player in this study.
- **Bill Evans**, "Alice in Wonderland" (Vanguard, 1961): he lays out under the bass solo (1.4). On "Someday My Prince Will Come" (*Portrait in Jazz*) the head is "staccato chords" (1.5, thin).
- **Bill Evans, "Waltz for Debby" (Vanguard, 1961), take 2.** The head is in 3/4, with LaFaro mostly on dotted halves. The solo pages of S2's transcription are in **4/4**: four beats a bar, Motian's hi-hat on 2 and 4. S2's own Waltz for Debby examples are also notated in 4/4 (thesis p. 30, "Chorus C Bars 7-8", `44`). On this recording the solos swing in four, so it is **not** evidence for 3/4 comping. (solid: transcription)
- **McCoy Tyner, "My Favorite Things" (1960).** The sources disagree on the meter. S11 (Hein) calls it "the paradigmatic jazz waltz … in 3/4 time" and says "The groove's main feature is the accented 'and' of one". S12 (Anderson) says the quartet's version is "brisker and 'bouncier,' justifying White's notation of the time signature as 6/8 rather than 3/4", and that a "6/8 time feel is established" at 0:09. S11 says Steve Davis "drones his open E string for nearly the entire thirteen minutes". Neither source notates Tyner's figure in a form I could read. (thin)
- **Herbie Hancock, "Footprints."** S13: first recorded on Shorter's *Adam's Apple* (1966), then on *Miles Smiles*. It is "often written in 3/4 or 6/8" and is "not a jazz waltz because the feel alternates between simple meter and compound meter". On *Miles Smiles*, S13 says, the band works between 12/8 (or 6/8) and 4/4. The Real Book writes it in 6/4 according to a forum post I did not fetch. It is not 3/4 waltz evidence. (thin)
- **Red Garland, Tommy Flanagan, Kenny Barron, Brad Mehldau:** I found no source on their waltz comping. S1 describes Garland's comping in 4/4 only.

### Q6. Drums

**6.1 (solid: an academic article plus a teaching page, with S7 agreeing in part).** **The ride: `x . x x x .`** (1, 2, the "and" of 2, 3). S3 (Butterfield) calls it "one of the common ride cymbal patterns for a jazz waltz", Example 3: quarter, quarter, then two eighths on 2 and its "and", then a quarter on 3. S6: "The basic jazz waltz or 3/4 swing pattern consists of 'one, two, and-of-two, three' … on the ride cymbal".
S7 offers a different practice pattern, "1, 2-and, 3-and" (`x . . x . x`), and adds "That is only one useful practice pattern, not a rule". It also says other patterns "lift the and of 2, beats 2 and 3".

**6.2 (solid on beat 2; the variants are thin).** **The hi-hat foot on beat 2.** S6: "with the hi-hat on 'two'. Other variations include using the hi-hat on 'two' and 'three', or on all three beats". In S2's transcription of Motian on "Alice in Wonderland", the hi-hat foot is on beat 2 in most bars of the head (thesis p. 120), and it often falls on 2 under the piano solo, with extra dotted-eighth and sixteenth figures in some bars.

**6.3 (solid for this trio: a transcription plus S5).** **Motian with brushes.** In the "Alice in Wonderland" head (S2, p. 120), the brush and cymbal stroke lands on **beat 2** together with the hi-hat. Under the piano solo and the bass solo (pp. 126, 135), a stroke falls on **all three beats**, with snare-staff notes and figures on 1 or 3 and off-beats varying bar to bar. S5's "the drums are accenting beat two" (on Evans's "Someday My Prince Will Come") matches the head pattern.

**6.4 (thin).** On the Kelly record S1 says only that the 3/4 is heard "in Cobb's brush pattern". The drums are not transcribed.

**6.5 (thin).** **Elvin Jones.** S10: "Elvin Jones plays this Afro Waltz – a jazz waltz pattern with Afro-Cuban influences – on many different recordings … You can really hear his signature groove in the dotted quarter notes on the bass drum." No recording is named and no full pattern is given.
I found no published source on Philly Joe Jones in 3/4. S17 lists 3/4 hi-hat riffs as a subject but its text is paywalled. S18 has a chapter on 3/4 comping that I could not read.

### Q7. Bass

**7.1 (solid: transcription).** **Chambers on the Kelly record:**
- Intro and first A (bars 1-44): three quarters a bar on an F pedal (`x . x . x .`).
- From bar 45 onward, mostly a dotted half on every downbeat (`x - - - - -`). S1 annotates bar 45: "Chambers starts playing a long root every downbeat". This continues through the whole first solo chorus (bars 69-100).
- Many bars carry a pickup: a half note tied into two eighths on 3 and the "and" of 3, the second accented (`x - - - x X`, bars 58, 62, 63, 72, 94, 95), or a half then a quarter on 3 (`x - - - x -`, bars 78, 90, 92).
- Bars 85-92, in the solo's second half, move more: half plus two eighths, or a dotted quarter plus eighths.

So on this record the bass goes **from walking to playing in one** at the bridge of the head, and stays in one under the trumpet. That is the opposite of the head-in-one, solos-walking order that S8 implies.

**7.2 (solid: transcription).** **LaFaro on "Alice in Wonderland":**
- In the head he plays mostly dotted halves and halves, with eighth-note pickups (S2 p. 120).
- Under the solo he plays broken time: mostly halves and quarters with pickups, three-beat patterns, and phrases that "completely [avoid] landing on beat one" for six bars (S2 thesis text, "Chorus A Bars 4-9").
- He plays one 25-bar near-walking passage, "the closest example of LaFaro nearly playing a traditional walking bass line, but for the dotted crotchet followed by a quaver in every second bar" (`x - - x . .` every other bar).
- He uses dotted-quarter polyrhythms "sparingly" and briefly.

**7.3 (thin).** S8 defines the three feels: "a dotted half note on every downbeat" (in one), "two dotted quarter notes in a bar" (the second on the "and" of 2), and "Walking in 3 is a note on every beat". It says moving between them "builds and releases energy", like two-feel and four in 4/4, and that Chambers "keeps a long, smooth waltz under the soloists". S7 says the bass "may walk in three, play a more spacious one-feel, or alternate between strong downbeats and moving quarter notes". S5: "The bass often outlines all three beats".

## What the sources do not say (the builder must not fill these by guessing)

- **Velocity.** Only relative markings exist: accents and marcato on single strikes (the `X`s above), "louder dynamic" at the end of an 8-bar phrase (S1, bar 52), and "mezzo piano" left-hand voicings under a busy soloist (S1, p. 28, 4/4 context). No numbers exist.
- **How often each figure occurs across a performance.** The rates in 1.1 and 1.2 are my own count of 16 + 7 bars of one recording. They show proportions on that record, not a population.
- **The rate of anticipation on the "and" of 3**, and how it compares with 4/4's "and" of 4. One tied instance is all I found.
